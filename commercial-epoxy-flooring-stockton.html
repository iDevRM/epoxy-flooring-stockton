What Does a Commercial Epoxy Install Involve?

The prep is the same idea as a garage, at a different scale. Ride-on and walk-behind grinders with dust containment open the slab to a CSP 1 to 2 profile. Joints get cleaned and filled with a semi-rigid filler so forklift wheels stop chipping the edges, and spalled areas get rebuilt.

Commercial systems are built thicker than residential ones. A high-build epoxy carries the impact and abrasion of pallet jacks and forklifts, and where the floor sees chemicals or wash-down we specify a resin rated for it. Anti-slip aggregate gets broadcast into the topcoat anywhere water shows up — wash bays, entries, dock aprons.

Line striping, aisle marking and safety-color zones go in before the final clear coat so they wear with the floor instead of off it.

When Do You Need Commercial Epoxy Flooring?

  • The slab is dusting and that dust is landing on product or equipment
  • Joints and edges are spalling and wrecking forklift and pallet jack wheels
  • You have a slick spot near a wash bay or entry that is a liability today
  • An inspection or client walkthrough is coming and the floor is the weak point
  • Chemicals, oil or solvents are staining and softening bare concrete
  • You need aisles, zones and safety markings that survive traffic

Why Do Commercial Floors Fail Faster Than Residential Ones?

Load and volume. A residential garage sees one or two vehicles parked on a coating; a warehouse floor takes point loads from loaded pallet jacks, hard forklift wheels, dropped freight and constant turning — the twisting motion is what tears at a thin coating.

The second reason is that commercial slabs are far more likely to be machine-troweled, which leaves a weak layer of cement paste sitting on the surface. Acid etching barely touches it. Coat over that layer and the floor delaminates in traffic lanes first, because that is where the shear is highest.

Moisture is the third. Large slabs poured on grade in the Central Valley move vapor upward, and a coating with no vapor tolerance traps it. The result is bubbling and disbonding in patches that look random but are not.

Epoxy vs. Polished Concrete: Which Do You Need?

Polished concrete is the existing slab mechanically ground and densified — no coating on top. Nothing can delaminate because there is nothing added, it handles moisture vapor without complaint, and it is the lower-maintenance option long term. It cannot give you color, chemical resistance, or a sealed non-porous surface.

Epoxy gives you all three. It seals the concrete against oil and chemicals, comes in any color with markings built in, and can be built thick enough to level a rough floor. It also depends completely on the bond, which means prep and moisture testing are not optional.

Rough guide: a food, pharma or chemical environment that must be sealed and cleanable wants epoxy. A dry warehouse or showroom where the concrete is already sound often does better with polish. If the slab is pushing moisture, that pushes the decision toward polish or toward epoxy over a vapor-barrier primer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you coat our warehouse floor without shutting us down?

Yes. Commercial work is normally phased section by section, or run over nights and weekends, so one area cures while you keep operating in the rest. We map the phasing with you before the quote so the schedule is part of the number, not a surprise later.

How long before forklifts can run on a new epoxy floor?

Light foot traffic is usually fine the next day. Full forklift and pallet jack traffic should wait for the resin to reach hardness, which is roughly five to seven days for a standard epoxy system. Faster-curing topcoats can shorten that when downtime is the binding constraint.

Does commercial epoxy hold up to forklift traffic?

A high-build system does, provided the slab was ground properly first. Failures in traffic lanes almost always trace back to prep: a machine-troweled slab leaves a weak layer of cement paste on the surface, and the twisting load of a turning forklift shears the coating right off it.

Can you add safety markings and aisle striping?

Yes. Aisles, safety zones and equipment markings go down before the final clear coat, so they are sealed under it and wear with the floor rather than off the top of it. They are priced by linear foot and quoted separately from the floor area.
